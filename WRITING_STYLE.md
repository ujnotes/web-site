# Ujnotes Writing Style

This guide describes the recurring voice and structure of the existing Ujnotes philosophy articles. Use it as a direction, not a rigid template.

## Voice

- Write plainly, directly and personally.
- Sound like a person thinking through an idea, not like a textbook or a research paper.
- Prefer everyday words over academic terms.
- Be confident when stating a conclusion, but distinguish facts, inference and personal judgement.
- Use `I` when a conclusion is personal. Address the reader as `you` only when it makes an example clearer.
- Let the concern for life, truth, reason, compassion and the wider world appear naturally when relevant.
- Use natural Indian/British English such as `centre`, `learnt`, `towards` and `behaviour`, while preserving established names and quotations.

## Article shape

1. Begin with the basic question, usually as a heading: `What is ...?`
2. Answer it immediately with a short definition.
3. Explain the idea in simple language.
4. Give an ordinary example or analogy.
5. Use a short list when the idea has clear types, parts or consequences.
6. Ask the next useful question: `How does it work?`, `Why does it matter?` or `What follows?`
7. End with a short conclusion, implication or action.

Not every article needs every step. Stop when the idea is clear.

## Paragraphs and sentences

- Keep paragraphs focused on one point.
- Prefer short and medium-length sentences.
- Break a long explanation into separate sentences or paragraphs.
- Put the main statement first; qualify it afterwards if needed.
- Use concrete examples: dark clouds before rain, a battery removed from a toy, an organ as part of a body.
- Repeat an important idea only when the repetition adds force or clarity.
- Use rhetorical questions sparingly and only when they move the reasoning forward.
- Use quotation marks for a word being questioned or used in a special sense, not as decoration.

## What to avoid

- Academic padding such as `at its core`, `underpins`, `functional transformation`, `latent engine` or `navigate ambiguity` when simpler words work.
- Several abstract nouns in one sentence.
- Restating the same conclusion in different polished-sounding phrases.
- Generic motivational endings.
- Long introductions before defining the subject.
- Claims that sound certain when the evidence only makes them probable.
- An impersonal, encyclopaedic or obviously AI-generated voice.

## Notion and NCMS formatting

- Use Heading 1 blocks for the main questions. NCMS renders them as article section headings.
- Use ordinary paragraph blocks for the explanation.
- Use bulleted or numbered lists only for genuinely separate items.
- Use bold for a term at the beginning of a list item; use italics for light emphasis.
- Add links where they help the reader continue through Ujnotes or check a source.
- Use special emoji callouts only for their documented NCMS purpose.
- Do not add `Component_bottom.php`, Facebook comments or Facebook buttons to the Notion page. NCMS appends `Component_bottom.php` automatically, and that fragment already provides the updated date, sub-list, Facebook comments, like/share buttons and navigation.

## Editing checklist

- Is the subject defined in the first section?
- Can an unfamiliar reader understand every sentence?
- Is there a simple example?
- Can any sentence be shortened without losing meaning?
- Are fact, inference and opinion clearly separated?
- Does each paragraph add a new point?
- Does the ending say something useful rather than merely sounding final?
- Will the NCMS blocks render without raw layout or social-component code?
